title: "Category Theory: Definition of a Category"
---

Across mathematics, many kinds of structures arise:

- Sets and functions between them.
- Groups and group homomorphisms between them.
- Types and typed terms representing transformations between them.
- Topological spaces and continuous maps between them.

What is common to all of these structures is that they consist of _objects_ and some sort of directed relationships (_arrows_) between them.
Adopting a birds-eye view of these structures and considering these arrows and objects as black-boxes is the starting point of category theory.
Not all associations of objects and arrows (_morphisms_) form a _category_.
The minimal structure of a category is:

- **Composition Exitence** For all morphisms $$f : A \to B$$ and $$g : B \to C$$, there exists a morphism from $$f \circ g : A \to C$$.
  Note that in the bird's-eye view of category theory, the composition of two morphisms is not defined, only that it exists and is unique.
  Rather, composition is defined in the close-up view of the category.
- **Associatitivity Law** For all morphisms $$f : A \to B$$, $$g : B \to C$$, and $$h : C \to D$$, the composition is associative: $$(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$$.
- **Identity Existence** For all object $$A$$ in the category, there exists an identity morphism $$\mathrm{id}_A : A \to A$$.
- **Identity Law** For all morphisms $$f : A \to B$$, the identity morphisms act as neutral elements for composition: $$\mathrm{id}_A \circ f = f = f \circ \mathrm{id}_B$$.
